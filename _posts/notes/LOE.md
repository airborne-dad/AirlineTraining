# ✈️ LOE Debrief

## 📅 Date
*2025-04-16*

# 🧾 LOE Summary

The LOE began with the usual paperwork and a pre-briefing session. The examiner, who had reviewed our logbooks earlier in training, brought a relaxed and friendly demeanor that helped calm nerves right from the start. Since both of us were on the unrestricted 1500-hour tracks, the paperwork went smoothly, though some tech delays extended the briefing to the full two hours.

I was assigned the first scenario and received the release before the break, which gave me a few minutes to review fuel, route planning, and briefing materials on the iPad. The aircraft was already powered up, the scenario is that it is the second flight of the day, so we skipped the safety and power-on checklists and jumped right into the originating and receiving flows.

The flight was from O’Hare to Cincinnati. ATIS reported active runways 22L and 27L/TT, and given our gate location at Bravo 22, we determined 27L at Tango Tango made the most sense. We briefed this as well as a low-visibility threat. The captain gave a thorough threat brief, and it became clear that I could be more detailed when it comes to airport-specific procedures and company references—something I’ll continue working on.

Departure went smoothly without any malfunctions. While preparing to call for flaps one, the captain reminded me to keep altitude restrictions in mind. We delayed the call until cleared, then continued the takeoff flow and checklist. The departure and climb instructions were straightforward. I chose to switch from purple to green, my trusted method to climb when unrestricted.

Enroute, we were given a couple of direct-to clearances and used that opportunity to brief the approach early, prompted by the captain. We encountered turbulence mid-flight but received a ride report from ATC recommending smoother air 2000 feet above. I made the decision to climb, and it worked out well. The captain took the lead on communicating with the flight attendants, which I appreciated and had planned to request. On the debrief, both the examiner and the CA mentioned that instead of staying at 310kts, we should've first slowed it down to 270 for smoother penetration. The best way to achieve that would be a flight level change climb. 

Later, we received a holding instruction at a published fix. I initially asked the captain to build the hold, but he encouraged me to take the task on myself—a fair reminder that I had the capacity to manage it. I inserted the hold into the FMS, double-checked the new speed and altitude with the CA, and monitored our descent with a 3° FPA while managing other tasks.

Fuel planning became a topic when we calculated our bingo fuel and noticed some differences in methodology. After discussion, we agreed on a conservative number and determined we could hold for less than 15 minutes, less than our EFC time. During our debrief, the CA mentioned that, although it's not our fault since we were taught to do it a particular way in training, when we go on the line, we should use rough estimation to quickly get a ball park number. Never be head down too much and become fixated on the numbers. Fly the plane, make safe decisions.

Soon after, we were notified of a ground stop at Cincinnati, and I coordinated with the captain to contact dispatch. I evaluated alternate options, identified Indianapolis as a feasible alternate using fixed info, and confirmed we were within range. After checking weather and confirming conditions were acceptable, we began planning the diversion.

During setup for the new approach, we experienced an autopilot failure. I disengaged the autopilot and took over hand flying. Unfortunately, I came in too fast on short final during the first approach, received multiple overspeed warnings, and decided that a go-around would be the best way to mitigate an unstable approach. On the second attempt, I focused heavily on managing speed. Though the controller kept us fast initially, I was able to get the aircraft slowed and stabilized enough for a safe landing. The flight director remained jumpy on short final, but I maintained control. The landing was slightly firm but within acceptable limits.

Taxi to the gate was routine. During the post-flight discussion, the examiner asked how I would handle the autopilot failure. I responded that I would have the captain write it up and notify maintenance, then wait for further instructions. He mentioned, during debrief, that we should consider doing a ground reset procedure first before talking to Mx as specified in the FOM. When asked what to tell the passengers, I said we’d inform them of the diversion and that gate agents would assist with rebooking to Cincinnati.

The LOE overall is a very fast-paced flight. On the debrief, the CA said he essentially kept us on a normal speed when operating on the line and was delightly surprised that boht of us didn't fall behind. The examiner said we both did a great job managing these tasks in a fast-paced environment and told us that we finished in record time without having to repeat anything. This is definitely another confidence booster in addition to the MV. We then went over some practical tips when going on line and talked about what IOE is going to look like for us. Can't wait to go out there and have some fun!

## 🛬 Diversion & Decision-Making

- Initiated diversion to IND **before verifying weather**.
  - Captain prompted weather check.
  - Took ~30 seconds to confirm chart suitability for approach.
- ✅ **Lesson Learned**: Validate with charts and METARs. Good to confirm for yourself, not just rely on others.

---

## ⚠️ Autopilot / Trim Failure

- Identified failure and made timely call.
- **High-speed base** approach setup led to:
  - Situational awareness drop.
  - Flaps 1 + speedbrake before gear.
  - Steep dive to intercept glidepath.
  - Multiple overspeed warnings.
- Called **go-around** after realizing the approach was unstable.
- ✅ **Lesson Learned**: Gear first. Prioritize energy management. Don’t press a bad setup.

---

## 🔁 Second Approach & Landing

- **Improved** situational awareness.
- Still struggled with **trim**—could’ve used backup trim earlier.
- Landing was **acceptable** but felt rushed.
- ✅ **Lesson Learned**: Practice managing stress and focus under load. Better use of trim tools.

## ✅ Final Takeaways

- Master FMS routing and rebuilds.
- Keep briefings clear, relevant, and threat-driven.
- Don’t over-delegate—trust your training.
- Validate weather and approach suitability yourself.
- Maintain composure under pressure—especially during go-arounds.
- Use CRM effectively; work with the captain.
- Finish strong—flows and post-flight comms were solid.
